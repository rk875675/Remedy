Is walking good for back pain?

Walking is free, needs no equipment, and has some of the most encouraging recent evidence of any activity for people who keep getting back pain.

By the Remedy team · Published October 23, 2026 · 2 min read · 5 sources

On this page

  3. How much to walk
  4. Walking plus a routine
  5. When to stop

Key takeaways

  • Staying active is better than bed rest for most back pain.
  • In a 2024 trial, a progressive walking and education program roughly doubled the time people went before their next episode of low back pain.
  • Build up gradually. Walking works well alongside a strength and mobility routine.

What the research says

The clearest recent evidence comes from the WalkBack trial, published in The Lancet in 2024. Adults who had recently recovered from an episode of low back pain were split into two groups. One followed an individualised, progressive walking program with education sessions. The other got no intervention. The walking group went a median of 208 days before their next episode, compared with 112 days for the control group 1.

That fits older evidence. A Cochrane review found people advised to stay active did slightly better than people advised to rest in bed 2, and a review of prevention studies found that exercise, alone or with education, lowered the risk of a new episode of back pain 3.

Why walking works

  • It is gentle, rhythmic movement that most people can do even with a sore back.
  • It is easy to make slightly harder over time by adding minutes or pace.
  • It needs nothing but shoes, so it is easy to keep doing, which is what matters most.

How much to walk

The WalkBack program built people up gradually toward about 30 minutes, five times a week, with the plan adjusted to each person 1. A practical way to start:

  1. Walk for as long as feels manageable, even 10 minutes, at an easy pace.
  2. Add a few minutes each week, not each day.
  3. Once 30 minutes feels easy, pick up the pace or add a gentle hill.

The NHS has more general advice on walking for health 4.

Walking plus a routine

Walking covers general activity. It does less for hip strength, core stability, and mobility, which is where a short routine fits in. A glute bridge, a few cat-cow stretch reps, and a hip flexor stretch make a good partner for a daily walk. The lower back exercise guide has a full starter routine.

When to stop

Stop and get checked if walking brings on pain that spreads down a leg with numbness, tingling, or weakness, or if your back is getting steadily worse over a couple of weeks 5. See the warning signs guide for the full list.

Questions

How much should I walk for back pain?

Start with what feels manageable, even 10 minutes, and build up gradually over weeks. In the WalkBack trial, people worked up toward about 30 minutes, five times a week.

Is it better to walk or rest with a sore back?

For most everyday back pain, staying active is better. People advised to stay active do slightly better than those advised to rest in bed.

Can walking make back pain worse?

It can if you do far more than you are used to all at once. Increase time and pace gradually, and stop if pain spreads down a leg or you get numbness or weakness.

When to see someone instead of using an app

Get medical care, not an exercise routine, if you have back pain with any of these:

  • Numbness or tingling around your genitals or buttocks
  • Trouble peeing, or loss of bladder or bowel control
  • Numbness, tingling, or weakness in one or both legs that is getting worse
  • Back pain after a fall, car accident, or other injury
  • A fever, or feeling unwell, along with back pain
  • Unexplained weight loss, or a history of cancer
  • Pain that is severe, constant, and gets worse at night or when lying down
  • Pain that keeps getting worse over several weeks instead of better
