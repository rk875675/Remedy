# Sore back after the gym: soreness or a tweak?

Most back pain after training is either normal soreness or a minor tweak. Telling them apart decides whether you train through it, train around it, or get it looked at.

Key takeaways

- Soreness shows up a day or two later, feels dull, and eases as you warm up. A tweak usually starts during the session and feels sharper.
- Keep training: swap the movement that provokes it, drop the load, and keep the pattern.
- Pain down the leg with numbness or weakness, or pain after impact, needs a clinician.

## What does normal soreness feel like?

Muscle soreness after a new or harder session usually shows up the next day or the day after. It feels dull and achy, is often on both sides, and eases as you warm up and move. It is a sign you did something your body was not used to, not a sign of damage.

## What does a tweak feel like?

A tweak tends to announce itself during the session: a sudden twinge on a deadlift, a squat, or a sloppy rep at the end of a set. The pain is sharper, often in one spot, and certain movements, like bending to tie a shoe or getting out of a car, catch it. Most tweaks settle on their own over days to a few weeks.

## Keep moving, change the load

Bed rest is not the answer for most back pain. The NHS [1] and the NICE guideline [2] both recommend staying as active as you can, and people advised to stay active do slightly better than those told to rest [3]. For a lifter, that usually means:

- **Swap, don’t stop.** Replace the movement that provokes it, like heavy hinges or loaded flexion, with something that does not, and keep training everything else.

- **Drop the load, keep the pattern.** Light, pain-free reps of a [hip hinge](https://remedyrecoveries.com/exercises/bodyweight-hip-hinge) are often fine long before your working weight is.

- **Rebuild the basics.** Core stability work like the [bird dog](https://remedyrecoveries.com/exercises/bird-dog) and [side plank from the knees](https://remedyrecoveries.com/exercises/side-plank-knees), plus glute strength from the [glute bridge](https://remedyrecoveries.com/exercises/glute-bridge) and [barbell hip thrust](https://remedyrecoveries.com/exercises/barbell-hip-thrust), gives your back a better platform when the heavy work comes back.

- **Come back gradually.** Add weight back over weeks, not one session. [Why weeks, not days](https://remedyrecoveries.com/guides/how-long-to-stick-with-a-back-routine) is its own guide.

If you are training at home while it settles, [a no-equipment back routine](https://remedyrecoveries.com/guides/back-exercises-no-equipment) keeps the habit going without a barbell.

## When to get it checked

See a clinician if the pain travels down your leg below the knee with numbness, tingling, or weakness; if it followed a fall or heavy impact rather than a lift; or if it is getting worse after a couple of weeks instead of better. And if you wake up locked up every day after training, [the morning stiffness guide](https://remedyrecoveries.com/guides/morning-back-stiffness) covers what is normal and what is not. The full list of warning signs is in [when to see a doctor](https://remedyrecoveries.com/guides/when-to-see-a-doctor-for-back-pain).

## Questions

### Should I work out with a sore lower back?

Usually yes, with changes. Keep training what doesn’t provoke it, swap the movement that does, and use lighter loads for the pattern that hurts. Staying active generally beats resting.

### How long does a tweaked back take to heal?

Most minor tweaks from lifting settle on their own over days to a few weeks. If it is getting worse after a couple of weeks, get it checked.

### When should I stop deadlifting with back pain?

Pause heavy deadlifts while the hinge pattern itself is painful. Light, pain-free hinges are often fine well before your working weight is, so build back up from there over weeks.

## When to see someone instead of using an app

Get medical care, not an exercise routine, if you have back pain with any of these:

- Numbness or tingling around your genitals or buttocks
- Trouble peeing, or loss of bladder or bowel control
- Numbness, tingling, or weakness in one or both legs that is getting worse
- Back pain after a fall, car accident, or other injury
- A fever, or feeling unwell, along with back pain
- Unexplained weight loss, or a history of cancer
- Pain that is severe, constant, and gets worse at night or when lying down
- Pain that keeps getting worse over several weeks instead of better
