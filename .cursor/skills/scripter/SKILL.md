# Scripter

One founder video script. Doc `16FqQ9YiidQwNAEpGFA7D_VtOBOxfpOyQ12TbgKH1WHk`. Write on the tab he links, or the next empty numbered tab.

There is no template. Every line in this file is a check, not a sentence to fill in. If two scripts in a row have the same open, the same middle beat, or the same closer, the second one is wrong.

## Before you write

1. **Read what is already used.** Open every numbered tab and the FOUNDER UGC tab. List each study in their Source sections, plus every hook and every closer. Do not reuse any of them. If he says a study was used before, believe him and check the tabs.
2. **Get the format from a real video.** If he links one, copy its edit and its script structure: the hook type, how the study is introduced, where the visuals pop in, the caption style, the pacing, and how it ends. Use its structure, never its study or its photos. If he does not link one, find a real outlier first (below).
3. **Find what people actually say.** Read comments on outlier back-pain videos and threads. Use the phrases people repeat in their own words. Never invent a "common" thing. If you can't point to where people say it, cut it.

## Outlier references

A reference is a real TikTok or Instagram video you opened. A blog writeup, a listicle, or a "viral examples" article does not count.

For each one, write down the link, the account, the views, the likes, and the account's followers or normal views. An outlier gets several times the account's normal views. Use the browser. TikTok stats are in `#__UNIVERSAL_DATA_FOR_REHYDRATION__` (`playCount`, `diggCount`, `followerCount`). If you can't confirm a count, say so. Never round up or guess.

When the format is new, add it to the FOUNDER UGC tab: format name, two or three outlier links with counts, what makes them work, and the clip list for ours.

## Hook

Use a hook pattern that has been proven, and change it each time. Pick the one that fits the study's result:

- "The biggest mistake I made with my back..."
- "[X] won't help your [Y]."
- "The worst mistake you can make with [Y]..."
- "Your scan / doctor / stretch never told you..."
- or the hook type from his reference video.

The hook has to be about the viewer's problem by the end of its first sentence. It has to promise exactly what the study answers. If the result doesn't pay off the hook, change the hook or the study.

**Open the loop, don't close it.** Never say the verdict in the hook. "A stretch class won't stop your back from flaring up" gives the answer away, and viewers left at 5 seconds. Pose the choice or the stakes ("Pick one. A or B. One was four times as likely to flare up again."), then hold the answer until the result numbers.

**Stack four hooks in the first second:**

- **Sound:** a real sound in the take on frame 0 (a desk slap, a band snap), not an effect added later.
- **Picture:** something moving or snapping in on that sound.
- **Text:** a header that adds a second idea, never a repeat of the spoken line.
- **The open question.**

No warm-up words ("This is going to piss some people off"). The first word is content. The study setup never starts at second 5; the line after the hook raises the stakes.

## Study

Pick a study with a big, surprising gap. A good one gives a result like "33 out of 50 versus 5 out of 50." A tiny difference, or a result that only looks good in the paper's math, is the wrong study. If you would need to explain why the result matters, find another one.

- Use a real paper you read: PubMed, the journal, or a university summary. Put the link in Source.
- Before you say a number, say what was measured and on what scale ("they rated their pain 0 to 10, 10 being the worst").
- Say each group as what they actually did, in plain words: "kept doing what they were already doing," "got a shot of salt water and knew it wasn't medicine." Never use the paper's labels.
- Every spoken number has to match the paper exactly. Never say a group "stayed the same" or "didn't change" unless the paper shows that.
- If serious cases were left out, say it in one short clause.

## Words

Write at a 5th-grade level. Use short words he would say to a friend.

Banned in the teleprompter: usual care, placebo, control group, intervention, participants, randomized, cohort, spell, episode, recurrence, non-specific, significant, outcomes, efficacy, modality, and any label you made up for a group.

Every line has to follow from the line before it and make sense to someone who never saw the notes. Read each line out loud. If you'd have to explain a phrase, rewrite it.

Never write random everyday micro-moments, like keys in a bowl, a sock, a grocery bag, a laundry basket, or a stair landing. They're filler. Only use a real-life detail if it comes straight from the study or from what people say in comments.

## Length and shape

The whole video is 30 to 40 seconds, about 75 to 100 spoken words. Blank lines in the teleprompter are breaths.

The order is hook, then the study, then the result, then the lesson, then Remedy. How each part sounds comes from the reference video, not from the last script.

## Ending

End with one or two lines that say what the result means for the viewer, as a lesson. The lesson comes from the result. Don't add a new idea.

Then one subtle Remedy line that grows out of that lesson. Use "we built Remedy," never "I built." Don't list features or say "link in bio." Write it new each time. Never copy a closer from an earlier tab.

The Remedy line must not promise a result. Never say a pain number, pain-free, fixed, or gone. Never use heal, cure, treat, guaranteed, forever, free, or free trial. No cursing. Don't say the pain was fake. Don't call a three-group split a half. Never tell the viewer to do an exercise.

## On screen

Never a plain talking head. Something on screen has to change at least every 5 to 8 seconds. Mark each visual in the teleprompter notes at the exact spoken words where it appears:

- `[lower box: ...]` is a card or clip in the lower half, just under the captions.
- `[full screen: ...]` is a short cutaway for the single biggest moment.

Visuals have to be clean. Allowed:

- a crisp screenshot of the real paper (the PubMed or journal header)
- a simple graphic made in the edit: numbers, a 0–10 scale, or bars that build as he says each group
- his own clean, normal clip
- a screen recording of the Remedy app

- in the hook only, one or two Unsplash License photos of exactly what the hook names (for example a stretch class next to band training)

Never allowed: other stock photos, low-quality or blurry photos, handwritten or marker sheets, memes, or anything that asks him to fake pain, hunch, or hold his back.

Captions are short phrases in time with his mouth, styled like the reference video.

The hook is a real thing in the frame for the first second. He holds an object up to the lens, or a short clip he filmed plays full screen. Words on a card are not a hook. Do not open on a text card.

## Movement

He sits, already on the lens, and does one take for his face. Give him at most one small, natural move at the moment that matters, like a lean in on the big number. He never acts hurt. If a clip needs filming, it has to be normal and clean, and you list it.

## Before you paste

Check every one of these:

- the hook pattern is proven and isn't the last tab's
- the study is new, has a big gap, and pays off the hook
- every number matches the paper
- no banned words, and each line makes sense alone
- 75 to 100 words
- a visual cue at least every 5 to 8 seconds, all of them clean
- the ending is a lesson plus a subtle Remedy line that promises nothing

## Post

Title: one plain sentence about the viewer's problem. Not a tag, not the plot, not the score. Max 90 characters.

Caption: the same story as the teleprompter. 4 to 6 short sentences, about 80 to 140 words, at the same 5th-grade level. Give the exact study number once. Say who it's for and why it matters, then end on a question. Last line: `Download Remedy on the App Store — link in bio`. No other URL. No "Remedy is free."

Hashtags go on the next line. Use three or four broad ones. Include `#backpain`. Never `#deskjob`. Never use a tag for an idea the video said was wrong.
